Not even counted

The most-used animals are often the ones nobody counts.

In the United States, mice, rats and birds bred for research are left out of the Animal Welfare Act's definition of "animal",6 so they don't appear in the official yearly numbers at all, even though they are the large majority of animals used in labs.

Better science

The future ishuman-relevant.

New approach methods study human biology directly, often faster and cheaper, and they keep improving.

i.

Organs-on-chips

Tiny devices lined with living human cells that mimic how a lung, liver or gut actually works.

ii.

Lab-grown human tissue

Reconstructed human skin and eye tissues can replace rabbit tests for irritation, and are already in international test guidelines.

iii.

Computer models

Software predicts how a chemical or drug might behave in the body, using huge databases of existing results.

iv.

Organoids

Miniature 3D organs grown from human stem cells, used to study disease and test medicines on real human biology.

It's working

Change isalready here.

2004

Europe stops testing finished cosmetics

The EU bans testing finished cosmetic products on animals, the first step of a phased ban.4

2013

A full sales ban in the EU

From March 2013, cosmetics newly tested on animals can't be sold anywhere in the European Union.4

2022

The US drops a legal mandate

The FDA Modernization Act 2.0 removes the requirement that new drugs must be tested on animals, opening the door to alternatives.5

2023

Canada joins in

Canada bans cosmetic animal testing, bringing the number of countries with bans to 44.3

2025

A roadmap away from animal tests

The FDA announces a plan to phase out animal testing requirements, starting with antibody drugs, using organ-on-chip systems and computer models instead.5
